Abstract¶

EquityAgent combines financial information retrieval, NLP, and an iterative language-model research workflow. A LangGraph agent plans research, delegates tasks to specialists, gathers market, macroeconomic, financial filing, and news information, and synthesizes an equity brief. The news component benchmarks financial sentiment classification against keyword and supervised baselines. The agent component compares evidence-grounded generation with a no-tools baseline. Finally, an enhanced evaluation layer adds acceptance thresholds for accuracy and evidence, selective numerical cross-checking, structured revision feedback, and selection of the strongest qualifying draft. The experiments cover NVDA, JPM, and XOM and report classifier metrics, research quality, tool reliability, memory behavior, and evaluation decisions. All results below are produced by the executable analysis rather than inserted from a previous run.

Notebook organization: Part A examines data and sentiment modeling; Part B traces the complete agent workflow; Part C evaluates outputs, baseline comparisons, and the effect of quality gates.

Research questions and experimental design¶

  1. Financial NLP: How does an LLM sentiment classifier compare with keyword classification and TF–IDF logistic regression on a held-out set of labeled financial headlines?
  2. Research agent: How do an evidence-informed research process and its revision loop compare with generation from a single prompt without tools?
  3. Evaluation controls: Do explicit evidence and accuracy thresholds, plus constrained numerical cross-checks, change which generated drafts are considered acceptable?

The original and reviewed policies reuse the same evaluator model; the comparison isolates their acceptance rules, not two separately trained judges. Agent calls and external data sources are nondeterministic. Results reflect the data and model responses available at execution time.

A4. Classifier diagnostics: supervised baseline, few-shot prompts, agreement¶

Three checks on the zero-shot LLM classifier above:

  1. A supervised baseline. A pretrained model can be applied to a task by prompting it or by training on labeled data (Jurafsky & Martin, 2026, Section 9.4). TF-IDF with logistic regression, trained on the same train split, stands in for the second, so the LLM is compared with a model that learned from these labels and not only with keywords.
  2. Few-shot prompting. Two demonstrations per label are added to the prompt, drawn from the train split, as Brown et al. (2020, Section 2.4) draw conditioning examples from the training set and never from the test set. Demonstrations come from sentences every annotator agreed on, and K is fixed in advance rather than tuned on the test set. The sentiment definition in the prompt now restates the PhraseBank annotation guideline: judge only what the sentence states, from an investor's view of the stock price (Malo et al., 2014).
  3. Majority vote across runs. The three runs per prompt are combined by majority vote, the self-consistency idea of keeping the answer most samples agree on (Wang et al., 2023). It reuses the runs already made, so it adds no API calls.

Finally, accuracy is broken down by annotator agreement. If the model is far more accurate where every annotator agreed, many remaining "errors" are sentences that people also labeled differently.

B7. Evaluator–Optimizer: generate, evaluate, and refine¶

The research agent synthesizes an initial brief, scores five criteria from 1 to 5 (completeness, evidence, accuracy, balance, and clarity), then uses specific evaluation feedback to revise the brief. Claude Sonnet 5 writes and optimizes the drafts; GPT-6 Sol evaluates them. These are distinct model roles, rather than two independent scoring models.

The baseline acceptance policy requires an average of at least 4.0 after the minimum revision count. The enhanced quality policy additionally requires accuracy and evidence scores of at least 4/5, blocks acceptance on recognized numerical contradictions, records unresolved figures, and can select the highest scoring acceptable revision.

Acceptance rule Baseline Enhanced policy
Minimum average 4.0 4.0
Minimum accuracy No separate floor 4 / 5
Minimum evidence No separate floor 4 / 5
Minimum revision rounds 1 1
Maximum evaluated drafts 3 3
Contradictions found by limited numeric parser Not a separate gate Block acceptance
Final draft choice Last evaluated revision Best accepted revision

Unrecognized or unsupported claims are unverified, not proven correct. The parser covers only a narrow subset of financial prose; discrepancies require human review. Exhausting the evaluation budget does not constitute acceptance.

Part C. Evaluation and comparative analysis¶

Evaluation and Iteration¶

The following experiments assess different aspects of the system, with limitations appropriate to each comparison. The no-tools baseline examines the value of access to research findings and the overall agent workflow; it does not isolate the revision loop. The memory comparison uses separate live agent runs and therefore cannot isolate the causal effect of memory from model and data variation. The policy replay applies original and enhanced acceptance criteria to the same sequence of observed rubric scores.

An LLM rubric supplies qualitative scores throughout. Numeric spot-checks are constrained by the parser and the formats of retrieved specialist data.

C1. Report quality, numerical evidence, and memory¶

Three descriptive analyses are reported:

  1. No-tools versus research agent. For each ticker, the baseline produces a brief without the specialist findings. Both briefs are graded against the same collected findings. This measures the combined effect of research evidence and agent workflow, not one component in isolation.
  2. Unsupported figure counts. A string-based check flags figures not repeated verbatim in findings. Rounding, scaling, and unit formatting can produce false positives; this measure is a review aid, not fact checking.
  3. Memory on versus off. Plans and rubric scores are compared across separate runs. Variation from LLM outputs and data availability remains a confounder.

The complete ticker comparison is also saved as a CSV to facilitate the accompanying written report. The computed table, rather than any earlier exploratory scores, is the source for the reported results.

C2. Paired Evaluator–Optimizer acceptance analysis¶

This is a controlled decision-policy ablation using the exact same recorded NVDA drafts and LLM rubric scores. Both acceptance rules rely on the same GPT-6 Sol evaluator, while Claude Sonnet 5 is responsible for draft creation and revision. The exercise compares stopping decisions; it is not a second independent live run or an experiment with a newly trained judge.

The baseline policy requires at least one revision and average score 4.0 or higher. The enhanced policy adds separate evidence and accuracy thresholds, a contradiction gate for recognizable financial claims, and retention of an acceptable earlier draft when a subsequent revision is worse. Changes in acceptance logic alone should not be interpreted as validated gains in financial correctness or investment performance.

C3. Limitations and threats to validity¶

  • Data provenance and currency. Market prices, news, filings, and macro series come from external APIs or caches and may vary by run date. Published financial statements and the agent's retrieved excerpts should be cross-checked before investment use.
  • Judgment variability. Rubric scores come from an LLM and can change between runs. Repeated evaluations and human ratings would be needed to estimate scoring reliability.
  • Numeric coverage. Only a subset of explicitly formatted financial claims is recognized. A zero-contradiction audit is not a claim that all figures are correct. Missing, ambiguous, or conflicting source evidence remains unresolved.
  • Comparison scope. The no-tools baseline differs in available evidence; the policy ablation shares observed scores; the memory comparison involves separate LLM runs. These answer different questions and should not be combined into one causal conclusion.
  • Benchmark leakage. A publicly available sentiment dataset may have appeared in model pretraining. PhraseBank accuracy does not establish robustness across current market news, languages, or evolving companies.
  • Application scope. Generated briefs are research aids, not verified investment recommendations. Human source review remains necessary.

Discussion and conclusion¶

EquityAgent demonstrates an end-to-end architecture connecting financial data retrieval, sentiment NLP, specialist research, structured synthesis, iterative assessment, and memory. Part A evaluates language understanding against baseline classifiers. Part B exposes each computational step and the evidence supporting the final report. Part C distinguishes descriptive model scores, source-grounding checks, and evaluator policy decisions.

The system combines a common five-criterion rubric with explicit quality thresholds, selective financial-claim checks, revision feedback, and selection of the strongest qualifying draft. These controls improve auditability; broader claims of financial accuracy require independent validation over more companies.
